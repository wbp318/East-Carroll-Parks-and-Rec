Jury duty guide

The judges of the 6th Judicial District Court, the East Carroll Parish Clerk of Court, and the jury management staff welcome and congratulate you as a fellow participant in the administration of justice. The right to a trial by a jury of one’s peers is a cornerstone of American justice. As a juror you will assist in keeping a system that is fair and gives the same opportunity to every citizen.

Quick facts. Report to the main courtroom on the second floor of the East Carroll Parish Courthouse at the time on your subpoena. Jurors are paid $25.00 per day plus mileage. Cell phones are not allowed on the second floor.
Why you may have to wait

Efforts are made to move cases quickly, but many matters must be settled before a jury is called. Witnesses may need to be located, attorneys may file motions, and other issues can delay calling the panel to the courtroom. The judges ask for your patience and will make your service as short and efficient as possible.

How jurors are selected

Once a year, the names of all licensed drivers and registered voters of East Carroll Parish are entered into the Clerk of Court’s computer system. The system removes duplicate names, then randomly selects the people who will receive a subpoena for jury duty.

Who qualifies

Under Louisiana law, a prospective juror must:

  • Be a citizen of the United States and of Louisiana, and have lived in East Carroll Parish for at least one year before service
  • Be at least 18 years old
  • Be able to read, write, and speak English
  • Not have a mental or physical infirmity that makes service impossible
  • Not be under indictment for a felony, or convicted of a felony for which they have not been pardoned

If you do not meet all of these requirements, contact the judge’s office to be excused. If jury service would cause undue or extreme hardship, you may contact the judge’s office to request a postponement or excuse.

Exemptions

Since April 28, 1994, Louisiana Supreme Court rules have eliminated group and occupational exemptions. You may claim an exemption if you:

  • Served on a jury or in the jury pool in the 6th Judicial District within the last two years, or
  • Are 70 years of age or older

You may still serve if you wish. If you claim an exemption, the Clerk of Court will help you present the necessary proof.

Your role as a juror

Report to the East Carroll Parish Courthouse at the time and date on your subpoena, in the main courtroom on the second floor. The assigned judge will determine whether you are qualified. Potential jurors may then be asked to wait in the old Police Jury room, also on the second floor. Please do not leave that area.

In the courtroom, please be respectful, alert, courteous, and honest about your feelings and opinions. If you are selected, pay attention at all times and follow the judge’s instructions. If you have a problem, such as not being able to hear a witness, or an urgent question, ask the bailiff to notify the judge.

After all the evidence and closing arguments, the judge will instruct you on the law. You will then be escorted to the jury deliberation room.

What happens during a trial
  1. Introduction. The judge tells you the names of the parties, their lawyers, and the nature of the case.
  2. Voir dire. The attorneys and the court question you to make sure you can be impartial. Attorneys may challenge a prospective juror for cause, and each side has a limited number of peremptory challenges that need no reason. A challenge is not personal; the attorney is using a right the law gives the client.
  3. The jury is seated. Some cases use a six-person jury and others a twelve-person jury.
  4. Opening statements. The plaintiff’s attorney (civil) or the District Attorney (criminal) explains what they intend to prove. The defense may also make an opening statement.
  5. Evidence. The side that brought the case presents witnesses, documents, and exhibits; the other side may cross-examine each witness, and then presents its own case. When an attorney objects, the judge rules on the question of law, sometimes after sending the jury out of the room.
  6. Closing arguments. Each attorney sums up the case. These arguments are not evidence.
  7. Instructions. The judge explains your duties, the law in the case, the possible verdicts, and their consequences.
  8. Deliberation. You choose a foreperson, who presides over deliberations and brings the verdict to court. The jury may be polled individually, and the verdict may be given in open court or by secret ballot viewed by the judge and attorneys. The Clerk of Court seals the ballots in the record.
  9. Dismissal. The judge thanks and dismisses you.
Petit and grand juries

A petit jury hears and decides cases. In criminal cases the State is the plaintiff and the accused is the defendant; criminal trials can involve traffic, misdemeanor, felony, and capital cases.

A grand jury does not decide cases. It has broad power to investigate criminal offenses and to examine the performance of public officials and institutions, and may charge a person with a crime to be tried before a petit jury. Its deliberations are secret and are conducted with the District Attorney or an assistant district attorney.

Pay, length of service, and dress

Pay. Jurors receive $25.00 per day plus mileage.

Length of service. The first day is normally limited to selecting juries for trials held that week. Jurors selected for a case return on the trial dates. Most trials last several days.

Dress. Proper attire is required. Shorts, men’s hats, muscle shirts, halter or midriff tops, and other inappropriate dress are not allowed. Cell phones are not allowed on the second floor of the courthouse.

Instructions for jurors who have been qualified
  • Write your juror number in the upper right-hand corner of your instruction sheet.
  • Each evening, call to find out which juror numbers must report the next day. If you are called, arrive at least 15 minutes before court begins so roll can be taken on time.
  • Do not discuss the case with other prospective jurors while you wait, and do not try to learn facts about the case on your own. All the evidence you need will be presented in the courtroom.
  • You may bring reading material for the waiting period, but no legal materials and nothing about the current trial.
  • Stay in the courtroom area during selection except for lunch and recesses. A bailiff will be present to help you.
  • Do not read, watch, or listen to news accounts of the trial.
  • The attorneys are under court order not to talk with you. This avoids any appearance of wrongdoing.
Who’s who in the courtroom, and common terms
Judge
An elected officer who presides over the court and administers the law.
District Attorney (D.A.)
The prosecuting officer who represents the State in criminal trials.
Lawyer (attorney, counsel)
The legal representative of a party in a trial.
Court reporter
The person who records and transcribes the official proceedings of a trial.
Bailiff
The official in each courtroom who attends to the security and comfort of the judges, jurors, and the court in general.
Bench
The seat occupied by the judge in court.
Bill of information
A formal charge of violation of a criminal statute made against a person by the District Attorney and filed with the Clerk of Court.
Challenge
During jury selection, attorneys for either side may ask the court to excuse certain people from a particular jury. A challenge for cause is made when an attorney believes a person is not appropriate for the case, for example someone who was recently a party to a similar suit or is related to the attorneys or parties; the judge decides. Each side also has a limited number of peremptory challenges, which the judge automatically grants.
Chambers
The private office of a judge.
Civil law
Disputes between individuals, corporations, other private entities, or public bodies such as the parish or the State, in which no violation of a criminal law is charged.
Criminal law
Law dealing with acts or omissions that a legislative body has identified as contrary to the public interest and to which criminal penalties are attached.
Cross-examination
Questioning of a witness by the party opposed to the one who called the witness, to test the truth of the testimony.
Defendant
In a civil action, the party being sued. In a criminal case, the person charged with violating a criminal statute.
Direct examination
The first questioning of a witness by the party who called the witness.
Expert witness
A person qualified by skill, training, or experience to speak with authority on a subject. The court decides whether a witness qualifies as an expert. An expert may offer opinions as well as observations.
Grand jury
A jury that inquires into complaints and accusations of criminal violations. It hears testimony and evidence and may bring charges by indictment. It does not decide guilt or innocence.
Indictment
A formal accusation by a grand jury that a person has violated one or more criminal statutes.
Instructions
The judge’s directions to the jury on the law that applies to the case and how to apply it to the facts.
Intervener
A person who voluntarily enters a case with the court’s permission.
Jury pool
A randomly chosen group of people from which jury panels are drawn.
Jury term
The length of time a citizen serves in a jury pool.
Minute clerk
A deputy clerk of court who administers the oath to jurors and witnesses and receives evidence as it is introduced.
Motion
A formal request by an attorney asking the court to take a specific action.
Objection
A lawyer’s protest against a procedure or action at trial, such as offering certain evidence, that the lawyer believes the rules do not allow. The judge sustains or overrules it.
Panel
A group of prospective jurors from which the jury for a particular case is chosen.
Petit jury
A jury that decides the facts and renders a verdict in a particular trial.
Plaintiff
In a civil action, the party who files suit.
Plea
A defendant’s answer to the charges.
Settlement
An agreement between the parties that ends a dispute without a trial.
Statute
A written law enacted by a legislative body such as a city council, the Legislature, or Congress.
Verdict
The jury’s formal decision. In a criminal case it concerns guilt or innocence; in a civil case, whether the plaintiff proved the case and, if so, the damages.
Voir dire
Questioning of prospective jurors by the judge and attorneys to decide whether each can fairly hear the case.
Witness
A person who testifies under oath about what they saw, heard, or otherwise observed.
